Week Topic Readings Notes
Module 1: Climate Science Foundations · Weeks 1–3

This module establishes the physical basis of the greenhouse effect and the history of its discovery and confirmation. By the end of Week 3, students should be able to interpret an observational temperature record and identify the forcing responsible for the observed trend.

Learning objectives

  • Explain Earth's energy balance and the carbon cycle.
  • Trace the development of climate science from Fourier's 1824 hypothesis through Hansen's 1988 congressional testimony to CMIP7.
  • Interpret observational climate data and distinguish forcing, feedback, and noise.

Guiding question: What evidence distinguishes anthropogenic warming from natural climate variability?

Module 2: Policy Landscape · Weeks 4–5

This module examines the institutional and political processes through which climate policy is formulated and implemented at the international and national levels.

Learning objectives

  • Describe the role of energy, water, and land use (AFOLU) in the structure of the modern economy.
  • Explain the structure of the UNFCCC, the Paris Agreement, and Nationally Determined Contributions.
  • Assess the function of the Enhanced Transparency Framework in holding countries to their commitments.

Guiding question: Given near-universal agreement that climate change requires a response, what accounts for the persistent difficulty of coordinated international action?

Module 3: Scenarios & Integrated Assessment · Weeks 6–8

This module covers the scenario-based tools used by researchers and governments to evaluate policy before implementation, including their methodological limitations.

Learning objectives

  • Explain the function of an SSP narrative and the rationale for using scenario families rather than single forecasts.
  • Use an integrated assessment model to trace the outcome of a mitigation pathway.
  • Connect carbon-pricing and fiscal mechanisms to national policy case studies.

Guiding question: A model's output is only as reliable as its underlying assumptions — which assumptions in these models warrant the greatest scrutiny?

Module 4: Energy–Water–Agriculture Nexus · Weeks 9–13

This module examines the energy, water, and agriculture sectors individually before analyzing their interactions and the trade-offs that arise when they are coupled.

Learning objectives

  • Model energy-system trade-offs across decarbonization pathways.
  • Quantify water stress and demand at basin scale, and relate these to questions of equity.
  • Analyze crop and land-use risk under a changing climate and its implications for food security.
  • Couple two or more sectors and identify cases in which improving one outcome imposes a cost elsewhere.

Guiding question: When a transition is introduced in one sector, how does that change propagate through the other two, and what does that imply for how a sectoral transition should be sequenced?

Rubrics

Class Engagement — 20%

Class Engagement is assessed on the basis of active participation rather than attendance, including questions raised during lecture, written responses to one-minute papers, and contributions to paper discussions. The evaluation criterion is evidence that assigned readings were completed and critically evaluated, not the frequency of participation.

Strong: Consistently raises questions grounded in the assigned readings; contributes substantively to paper discussions; one-minute papers demonstrate synthesis rather than summary.
Adequate: Responds appropriately when called upon; one-minute papers indicate that the assigned reading was completed.
Limited: Passive participation; one-minute papers are superficial, generic, or absent.

Assignments — 10%

Assignments are take-home. Use of generative AI tools is permitted, provided that the prompts used are submitted alongside the completed assignment, which allows evaluation of the reasoning process in addition to the final output. Assignments demonstrating critical engagement with an AI tool — for example, identifying and correcting errors in its output, or iteratively refining a query — are evaluated according to different criteria than assignments completed without AI assistance; neither approach is penalized by default. In both cases, the assessment criterion is demonstrated understanding of the submitted work.

Midterm & Final Exams — 20% each

Both examinations are open-book. The format — written, oral, problem-based, or a combination — is determined by the instructor and announced in advance of each examination; the format of the midterm and final examination may differ. In all cases, examinations require live reasoning through course material rather than recall or recognition.

Project — 30%

Milestones and defense format are detailed in the Project section. Grading evaluates both the team-produced artifact and each student's individual defense of that artifact; the precise weighting between these components will be announced with the milestone rubric early in the semester.

Course Philosophy on AI Use

Generative AI tools are permitted for take-home assignments on the condition of full disclosure: prompts used must be submitted alongside the completed work. This requirement exists to make the reasoning process visible for evaluation, not merely the final output. Assignments are not evaluated on whether AI was used, but on whether the submitted work demonstrates genuine understanding; the evaluation criteria for AI-assisted and non-AI-assisted work differ accordingly, reflecting the different evidence available in each case.

Class Engagement, both examinations, and the project defense are conducted synchronously and in person, where AI assistance is not available. These components provide a direct assessment of individual understanding.

Policies

Academic Honesty

LUMS maintains a zero-tolerance policy toward academic dishonesty. Students are responsible for upholding academic integrity; where ambiguity exists, students should consult the student handbook or contact the instructor or teaching assistant in advance of submission. Use of AI on an assignment without disclosure of prompts, as specified above, constitutes a violation of this policy. Essays may be checked for similarity via similarity@lums.edu.pk prior to submission.

Generative AI Policy

The application of this policy within the course is described under Course Philosophy on AI Use, above. The underlying university-level policy: AI tools are permitted for brainstorming, refining a research question, locating information, drafting an outline, and checking grammar and style. They are not permitted for submitting AI-generated content as one's own in discussion posts or live sessions, for completing group work assigned to an individual student, or for producing any part of a graded submission — from outline through final draft — without disclosure. Violations are treated as academic misconduct.

How to Approach the Project

Project selection should be based on genuine intellectual interest rather than convenience. Students should identify a question in energy, water, agriculture, or the interactions among these sectors that merits sustained investigation.

The research question should be formulated before a method or tool is selected. A well-posed question is falsifiable: by the conclusion of the project, it should be possible to state whether the evidence supports the question, contradicts it, or leaves it unresolved.

The choice of method should follow from the question rather than precede it. Appropriate methods include integrated assessment models (MESSAGEix, GCAM, or comparable frameworks), land-use tools such as FABLE, decision-science frameworks such as Robust Decision Making (the XLRM framework: Uncertainties, Levers, Relationships, Metrics), or alternative approaches including systems mapping, comparative policy analysis, or original data analysis. Methodological fit is the objective, not technical complexity for its own sake.

The project should conclude by returning to the original research question: what was found, the degree of confidence in the finding, and what evidence would alter the conclusion.

A subset of projects each year is selected for publication on this website as short public briefs.
