Notes

Evaporation — latent heat

Liquid water needs energy to become vapour: about 2.5 million joules per kilogram, taken from the surface it leaves. That energy is not lost, it is carried upward inside the vapour. When the vapour condenses into cloud, kilometres above the ground, the energy is released there instead.

So evaporation moves heat from the surface to the upper atmosphere without any radiation involved. It is a lift, not a staircase — the energy skips the queue. Globally it carries about 80 W/m², the single largest non-radiative transport, and it is the link between this lecture and the water cycle.

Convection — rising air

Air warmed by contact with the ground becomes less dense and rises, carrying its heat with it. Cooler air sinks to replace it. This is the same process as a boiling pot, operating over the whole troposphere.

Convection carries roughly 20 W/m² upward and, more importantly, it sets the shape of the temperature profile: about 6.5 K of cooling per kilometre of altitude. Radiation decides where the planet must sit at 255 K; convection decides how temperature falls between there and the ground.

Albedo — reflection is not absorption

About 30% of arriving sunlight is reflected straight back to space by clouds, ice and bright land. It bounces at the same wavelength it arrived and warms nothing on the way.

This is why the balance equations use 240 W/m² and not 340. Only absorbed energy has to be paid back. A mirror does not get hot.

Emissivity — why σ is universal but ε is not

The Stefan–Boltzmann constant σ applies to every object in the universe. What differs between materials is emissivity ε, between 0 and 1, in E = εσT⁴.

Earth's surface has ε ≈ 0.95 in the infrared, close enough to 1 that we drop it. Note that emissivity is wavelength-dependent: fresh snow reflects 80% of visible light and is still a near-perfect emitter in the infrared. "Black" means black at the wavelengths in question.

Optical thickness — why more layers keep working

If one layer already absorbs everything, how can a second one matter? Because the top of the atmosphere is pinned at 255 K by the Sun and cannot move. Adding layers does not change the top — it adds more steps between the top and the ground, and each step has a temperature drop across it.

More steps, deeper the bottom: Tₛ = (n+1)¼ × 255 K. This is why "the CO₂ bands are already saturated" is not an argument against further warming. Full absorption is assumed in this model from the first line, and the effect still grows with n.

Radiative forcing — the units behind RCP and SSP

A forcing is a sustained imbalance at the top of the atmosphere, measured in W/m². Doubling CO₂ gives about 3.7 W/m².

This is the same unit as the 340 and the 240 above. RCP8.5 means an 8.5 W/m² imbalance by 2100; RCP2.6 means 2.6. Under the AR6 names — SSP1-2.6, SSP5-8.5 — the trailing number is still the forcing.

Where this model is wrong, and why that is useful

Three named failures. The atmosphere is not perfectly absorbing: about 40 W/m² leaves the surface directly through the 8–12 µm window. Radiation is not the only transport: evaporation and convection move roughly 100 W/m². And the real atmosphere does absorb some sunlight on the way down, about 78 W/m², which this model sets to zero.

Bare rock is 33 K too cold. The perfect greenhouse is 15 K too warm. Reality sits between them — bracketed from both sides using nothing but conservation of energy.
